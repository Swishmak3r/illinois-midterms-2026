## People v. Doehring — 2024 IL App (1st) 230384

2024-09-30; Illinois Appellate Court, First District, Third Division. Published precedential opinion.

**Lampkin’s role:** Joined Justice Reyes's judgment and opinion; did not author a separate opinion.

**Decision:** Affirmed denial of collateral relief. The facial challenge to the FOID-related aggravated-unlawful-use-of-a-weapon provision failed because constitutional applications existed, including disarming people covered by Rahimi. The under-21 FOID challenge also failed. Dismissal without notice or an opportunity to respond violated due process, but was harmless because the legal claims could not succeed.

**Reasoning:** Applied Bruen and Rahimi. Rejected the State's argument that unlicensed carrying automatically falls outside Second Amendment protection; examined historical justification. The panel rejected procedural forfeiture arguments and reached the constitutional merits.

**Scope:** Facial validity of the specific FOID-related provision, not approval of every gun restriction or a blanket ruling that all young adults can be disarmed. The separate age-only offense was not before the court.

**Review history:** Appellate rehearing denied October 23, 2024 (official report cover). Illinois Supreme Court denied leave to appeal September 24, 2025, docket 131264, identifying First District case 1-23-0384. A discretionary-review denial is not a merits endorsement. Later federal review not verified.

Pinpoints: ¶¶12–15 (procedural objections); ¶¶23–28 (Second Amendment framework and facial challenge); ¶¶29–31 (age-related claim and its limits); ¶¶33–38 (due process; harmless error; disposition). Role: Official report cover, Panel.

Policy-comparison limitation: Must also disclose use of Bruen/Rahimi, recognition of protected conduct, facial-challenge threshold and the author's identity. This does not establish Lampkin's personal ideology or Trump support.

## People v. Watkins-Romaine — 2025 IL App (1st) 232479-B

2025-03-26; Illinois Appellate Court, First District, Third Division. Published precedential opinions; initial 2024 judgment reversed by Illinois Supreme Court.

**Lampkin’s role:** Authored both the initial 2024 opinion and the 2025 opinion after Supreme Court remand; D.B. Walker and Van Tine joined both.

**Decision:** On remand, affirmed detention after independent review of the parties' proffers. Alleged threats combined with circumstantial evidence satisfied the statutory proof, dangerousness and inadequacy-of-release-conditions requirements.

**Reasoning:** Applied the State's clear-and-convincing burden to all three detention elements and de novo review under People v. Morgan where the hearing relied only on proffers. Lack of prior felonies and bond forfeitures did not outweigh the particular alleged threat.

**Scope:** Pretrial detention sufficiency, not a determination of guilt or a ruling for or against cash bail as policy.

**Review history:** The 2024 appellate judgment was reversed. The latest merits opinion verified is the March 26, 2025 affirmance on remand. No later Watkins-Romaine disposition was found in the 101 official 2024–2026 PLA lists screened; this is not proof that all review or trial proceedings are concluded.

The chain must remain visible when presenting this case:

- 2024-03-18, 2024 IL App (1st) 232479: Lampkin's opinion reversed detention on statutory timing grounds and required a release-conditions hearing; did not decide evidentiary sufficiency. (¶¶27, 30–54)
- 2025-01-24, 2025 IL 130618: Illinois Supreme Court reversed. Majority held the State could respond to a request to reopen unsatisfied cash-bail conditions with a detention petition, then remanded the unresolved sufficiency issues. Lampkin was not a member of that court. (¶¶39–53; Rochford/O'Brien special concurrence ¶¶54–89 agreed with reversal on forfeiture/plain-error grounds but disputed the majority's statutory interpretation.)
- 2025-03-26, 2025 IL App (1st) 232479-B: Lampkin authored affirmance of detention under the Supreme Court mandate. (¶¶2–3, 23–44)

Pinpoints: 2025-B ¶¶23–24 (burden and review); 2025-B ¶¶28–41 (evidence, threat, release conditions); 2025-B ¶¶43–44 (disposition). Role: Both official report covers, Panel.

Policy-comparison limitation: Initial procedural release ruling and later detention affirmance must travel together. Following a Supreme Court mandate and evaluating alleged threats does not reveal a stance on cash-bail abolition or a general tough/soft-on-crime ideology.

## Eberhardt v. Village of Tinley Park — 2024 IL App (1st) 230139

2024-04-24; Illinois Appellate Court, First District, Third Division. Published precedential opinion.

**Lampkin’s role:** Authored majority opinion; D.B. Walker and Van Tine joined.

**Decision:** Affirmed dismissal with prejudice. An agenda-germaneness rule at special meetings satisfied reasonableness and viewpoint-neutrality requirements under the Illinois Constitution; regular meetings allowed other topics. Retaliation claims failed pleading requirements. The outside-counsel claim failed for taxpayer standing and, alternatively, lawful appointment/ratification.

**Reasoning:** The rule excluded off-topic comments across viewpoints. The opinion expressly recognized that state-law immunity cannot defeat a federal section 1983 claim. It found insufficient allegations of actionable retaliatory harm and, for some defendants, personal involvement.

**Scope:** Contains a substantive speech ruling plus pleading, preclusion and standing rulings. It does not authorize suppressing criticism because officials dislike a viewpoint, and it is inaccurate to describe the entire decision as only about standing.

**Review history:** Appellate rehearing denied May 14, 2024 (official report cover). Illinois Supreme Court denied leave to appeal September 25, 2024, docket 130759, identifying First District case 1-23-0139. A discretionary-review denial is not a merits endorsement. Later federal review not verified.

Pinpoints: ¶¶23, 25, 31 (pleading, forfeiture, preclusion); ¶¶42, 55–57 (speech standard and holding); ¶68 (federal civil-rights immunity limitation); ¶¶71–76 (retaliation pleading); ¶¶79–97 (standing and appointment). Role: Official report page 2, Panel.

Policy-comparison limitation: An ordinary municipal forum rule is not a ruling on presidential power, media censorship, political dissent generally, or Trump's conduct. Viewpoint discrimination remained prohibited.

## Maksym v. Board of Election Commissioners of the City of Chicago — 406 Ill. App. 3d 9 (2011), No. 1-11-0033

2011-01-24; Illinois Appellate Court, First District, First Division. Published opinion; appellate majority judgment reversed.

**Lampkin’s role:** Lampkin dissented. Hoffman authored the majority opinion and Hall joined it. Lampkin would have left Rahm Emanuel on the mayoral ballot.

**Decision:** Lampkin's dissent would have affirmed the electoral board and circuit court: established residency continued absent abandonment, and the board's finding of no intent to abandon was supported by the evidence. She rejected a newly imposed actual-presence standard.

**Reasoning:** Applied existing residency precedent, the challengers' burden to show abandonment and deference to supported electoral-board findings.

**Scope:** A candidate-specific statutory residency dispute. The dissent is not itself binding law; it says nothing about election fraud, certification of presidential results, or Trump eligibility.

**Review history:** On January 27, 2011, Illinois Supreme Court reversed the appellate majority and affirmed the circuit court/board, leaving Emanuel eligible. See 242 Ill. 2d 303, No. 111773, slip pp.18–21. Justices Freeman and Burke agreed with the result but disputed the majority reasoning and criticized the tone of both the appellate dissent and Supreme Court majority (pp.22–25).

Pinpoints: Appellate slip opinion pp.25–30 (position and abandonment analysis); pp.35–42 (statutory construction and consequences). Role: Official opinion page 1; dissent pages 25–42.

Policy-comparison limitation: A Democratic candidate benefited, but deciding his residency under established law is not an endorsement of his party. This predates Trump's presidency and concerns a different legal question from election denial or presidential immunity.
